THE CONCEPT / WHY DIFFRACTION HAPPENS

What is Bragg's law?

When X-rays encounter a repeating crystal structure, waves scattered from neighboring lattice planes can reinforce one another. Bragg's law gives the angles at which that constructive interference is possible.

nλ = 2d sin θ

λ is the X-ray wavelength; d is the distance between planes; θ is the angle between the incoming beam and those planes; n is the diffraction order. A powder diffractometer typically reports 2θ, the angle between the incident and diffracted beams.

What changes? At the same wavelength, smaller d means larger θ and larger 2θ. Bragg's law locates possible reflections; it does not calculate their intensities.

What this model means

Guided lattice view: the (10) and (11) families are drawn in a two-dimensional square lattice with parameter a. Their separations are d(10) = a and d(11) = a / √2. The illustrated dots are translational lattice points, not individual atoms or a real MOF structure.

θ is the angle between a ray and a lattice plane. A powder pattern normally reports 2θ. Here q = 4π sin θ / λ = 2πn / d. Wavelength and spacing are in angstroms; q is in inverse angstroms.

This predicts a geometric diffraction condition, not reflection intensity. Atomic positions, scattering factors, and symmetry determine intensities and systematic absences. The diagram does not predict a complete diffraction pattern or identify a phase. Higher-order diffraction is often represented by different Miller indices with n = 1.

The ray diagram uses a mirror-like construction only to explain path difference; actual diffraction results from interference among scattered waves, not specular reflection from atomically flat mirrors. No intensities, structure factors, detector peaks or complete PXRD patterns are computed.

Preset wavelengths are rounded educational values. No wavelength uncertainty, refraction correction, or instrumental calibration is applied.
